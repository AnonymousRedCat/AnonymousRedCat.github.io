Knots for the
Philmont Trail

Five knots that pitch your shelter, join your lines, and keep your gear where you left it.

Crew prep: learn them at home, tie them in the dark at Philmont.

Why knots matter out there

ShelterRain flies and dining flies need tight, adjustable lines when afternoon storms roll in.
Camp workClotheslines, packs, and gear lines all depend on knots that hold and then untie easily.
Cold fingersThe right knot is one you can tie by feel, wearing gloves, at dusk.

Square knot

Joins two ropes of the same thickness. Good for bundling and bandages, not for loads that matter.

  1. Hold one end in each hand.
  2. Cross the right end over the left and tuck it under.
  3. Cross the left end over the right and tuck it under.
  4. Pull all four strands snug. Both loops should lie flat and parallel.

Right over left, left over right.

Check itIf it looks twisted, you tied a granny knot, which slips. Start again.

Bowline

A fixed loop that will not slip or tighten, and still unties after taking a heavy load.

  1. Make a small loop in the standing part. This is the rabbit hole.
  2. Pass the free end up through the hole. The rabbit comes out.
  3. Take it behind the standing part, around the tree.
  4. Bring it back down through the hole and pull tight.

Up the hole, around the tree, back down the hole.

Use it for tying a line to a ring, a post, or a person who needs a safe loop.

Clove hitch

The quick way to start a lashing or tie a line to a pole, tree, or rail.

  1. Wrap the rope around the post.
  2. Cross over the first wrap and wrap around the post again.
  3. Tuck the end under the last crossing.
  4. Pull both ends to tighten.
Know its limitIt can work loose if the pull changes direction. Finish it with a half hitch or two for anything important.

Lark's head

The fastest way to hang a line from a ring, rail, carabiner, or pole. Also called a cow hitch.

  1. Fold the rope in half to make a loop at the middle.
  2. Lay the loop over the ring or pole.
  3. Pass both free ends back through the loop.
  4. Pull the ends to cinch it down.
Know its limitIt can slip if the pull is uneven or only on one end. Use it for light jobs, or back it up with a bowline.

Taut-line hitch

An adjustable loop for tent and fly lines. Slide it to change tension, and it grips when loaded.

  1. Pass the line around the stake or tree and back along itself.
  2. Wrap the free end around the standing line twice, working toward the tent.
  3. Take one more wrap on the outside of those turns, away from the tent.
  4. Snug it down, then slide it to set the tension.

Loose line after a rainstorm? Slide the knot back out. No retying needed.

Before you hit the trail

  • Pack 10 feet of cord and tie each knot ten times in a row.
  • Practice with eyes closed, then with gloves on.
  • Quiz your crew: name the job first, then pick the knot.
  • Pitch your fly at home using only these knots.

Be Prepared.

Knot race

Who in the crew can tie it fastest? Everyone gets a 3-foot piece of rope and a pole or ring to tie to.

Round 1: Square knotTie it, hold it up, and have a judge check it.
Round 2: Lark's headHang the rope from the pole.
Round 3: Clove hitchTie it to the pole.
Round 4: BowlineTie the loop around the pole.
Final: Taut-line hitchTie it on a stake and slide it tight.
  • Hands in the air until the leader says go.
  • The knot must be correct to count. A wrong knot means you retie.
  • The fastest correct knot in each round earns a point. Most points wins.
  • For a harder race, go with gloves on or eyes closed.
